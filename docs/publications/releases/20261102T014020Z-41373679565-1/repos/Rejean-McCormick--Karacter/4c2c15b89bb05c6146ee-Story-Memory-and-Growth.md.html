# Story Memory and Growth

Karacter distinguishes between **what might happen** and **what the author decides happened**.

## Make a reaction canonical

1. Generate reactions in **Simulate**.
2. Review and, if needed, edit a candidate.
3. Choose **Make canon** for the intended response.
4. Add the **outcome**, optional affected character/relationship and any notes.
5. Open **Story** to review, revise or remove the event.

A canonical event is an author-approved story fact. The engine may refer to recent approved events when generating later reactions, but it must not treat unapproved possibilities as memories.

## What is kept in a story event?

Depending on the authored choice, a story event may record the scenario, character, action, outcome, relationship effect, notes, creation time and references to the simulation run. The author can edit or remove an event.

## Memories influence; they do not overwrite identity

A past betrayal, for example, might be mentioned as context during a new trust decision. That does not automatically mean the character becomes "untrustworthy" or has their sliders moved.

**Polarities do not change automatically.** The author controls deliberate evolution.

## Apply growth explicitly

In **Understand → Studio**, you can create milestones describing an event, a prior belief, and a possible new belief. The **Apply growth** action intentionally updates the character's limiting belief and records the change. The **Undo growth** control can reverse the latest applicable change.

Example: King Klown might learn to **ask permission before influencing others**, while still retaining his playful, strategic nature. This is a suggested arc, not canon unless the author adopts it.

## Export and backup boundaries

The **Story** screen supports an exported story TXT for reading and sharing. An individual character TXT sheet does **not** include the entire shared story timeline. For durable preservation of canonical events, comparisons, relationships and artwork, use a **full ZIP or JSON backup**.

## When writing for children

Keep the emphasis on emotions, intentions and consequences, not labels or diagnoses. Encourage questions such as "What else could the character have done?" and "Would that choice work in a different situation?"
